Cross-Border Litigation Careers in New York: Notes from an AABANY Panel

Themes from the 2026 Legal Careers Panel at NYU School of Law, traced through one litigator’s path from large-firm training to a boutique practice.

By Angus Ni, Morrow Ni LLP, New York. November 10, 2026

In September 2026, New York litigator Angus Ni joined the 2026 Legal Careers Panel & Networking Event at NYU School of Law, co-hosted by the Asian American Bar Association of New York (AABANY), the NYU China Law Students Association and the Chinese American Lawyers Forum (CALF). The panel brought together senior lawyers from international law firms, boutique litigation practices and global financial institutions. Angus Ni, co-founder of Morrow Ni LLP, spoke on career planning, professional development and the realities of cross-border litigation practice in New York.

This piece sets out the themes behind those topics as they appear in Mr. Ni’s path from Debevoise to Moni Law, for law students and junior lawyers weighing a similar route.

Institutional training first

Mr. Ni’s route ran through two large firms before he co-founded his own. At Debevoise & Plimpton LLP he worked on ICC and ICSID arbitrations and multi-jurisdiction corporate investigations. At Bernstein Litowitz Berger & Grossmann LLP he prosecuted securities class actions for pension fund and hedge fund investors. Each setting taught a different discipline: arbitration and investigations demanded rigor across legal systems, while class action practice demanded command of large records and the economics of damages.

For students drawn to cross-border work, the lesson is that cross-border is not a practice area by itself. Clients engaged in transnational disputes need lawyers who are first strong in a core discipline, whether securities litigation, arbitration or trial work, and who can then apply it across borders.

Language as a legal skill

Fluency in Mandarin is often described as a résumé asset. In practice it is something more specific. A bilingual litigator can read the underlying documents in the original, notice when a translation flattens a meaning that matters, and prepare a witness for a deposition in the language the witness thinks in. Those are litigation tasks, and they reward the same precision as drafting a brief.

The value grows with the stakes. In complex commercial disputes, whether a client understands U.S. procedure can decide whether settlement opportunities are recognized and whether discovery obligations are met. Lawyers who can explain those mechanics directly, in Chinese, close a structural gap that many Chinese clients encounter when they enter the U.S. legal system.

Why New York

New York remains the center of U.S. financial markets and securities regulation, and its federal courts, including the Southern and Eastern Districts, are among the most active venues for the securities and cross-border commercial disputes that define this kind of practice. The city also has an established Chinese-American legal community, with organizations such as AABANY and CALF offering mentorship and the kind of ongoing guidance that a single career panel can only begin to provide.

Building a boutique practice

Leaving a large firm to co-found Morrow Ni LLP meant taking on matters with smaller teams and direct responsibility for outcomes. The work since then shows what that responsibility looks like, from a six-day FINRA hearing against J.P. Morgan to advising listed companies on the litigation exposure of a U.S. listing. Students who want to follow the firm’s work can find case announcements on the Moni Law homepage.

Pro bono work belongs in that picture too. Mr. Ni served as pro bono trial counsel in United States v. Zhu, a federal criminal case that ended in a judgment of acquittal. For junior lawyers, pro bono trials remain one of the few routes to early courtroom responsibility, and the preparation they demand is the same preparation that paid matters require.
