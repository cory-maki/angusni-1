Reg SHO Close-Outs and the J.P. Morgan FINRA Arbitration

How cross-examination of a broker-dealer’s own witnesses reduced a $6.4 million claim to the cost of a timely close-out.

By Angus Ni, Morrow Ni LLP, New York. September 15, 2026

When J.P. Morgan Securities LLC brought a FINRA arbitration against a customer over a short sale of a newly public company’s stock, it asked for more than $6.4 million on fraud and breach of contract theories. The customer was represented by Morrow Ni LLP, practicing as Moni Law, with a trial team led by New York securities litigator Angus Ni together with Serena Yang and Jing He. After a six-day evidentiary hearing, the panel denied the fraud claims in full and limited the customer’s exposure to $279,250, with each side bearing its own fees.

The gap between those two figures is the subject of this piece. It did not come from a technicality. It came from the record of what the broker-dealer knew, when it knew it, and what it chose to do next.

The contractual starting point

Broker-versus-customer arbitrations usually begin on favorable ground for the firm. Account agreements allocate the risk of a failed or unlocated short sale to the customer, and claimants typically present the resulting loss as a simple debit: the position was short, it had to be covered, and the cover cost what it cost. Customers in that posture often face significant contractual exposure, and many cases resolve on that basis.

J.P. Morgan’s claim followed that pattern. The short sale involved IPO stock, and the position raised issues under Regulation SHO, the SEC’s framework for short selling. Among other things, Regulation SHO requires a broker-dealer to have reasonable grounds to believe a security can be borrowed before it accepts a short sale order, and it requires firms to close out fail-to-deliver positions within set timeframes by purchasing or borrowing shares.

What the close-out would have cost

The defense centered on a narrow question with large consequences. If the position had been closed out when Regulation SHO contemplated, what would it have cost? Through cross-examination of JPMS’s own witnesses, the Moni Law team established that J.P. Morgan knew the compliant close-out cost was $279,250.

The firm did not close the position at that point. It left the position open and ultimately closed it during a historic spike in the stock’s price. That decision, not the original trade, is what carried the claimed damages above $6 million.

The panel accepted that framing. It held that every dollar above the $279,250 close-out cost was the direct and foreseeable consequence of J.P. Morgan’s own decisions.

Why the cross-examination mattered

Arguments about causation and the duty to mitigate are familiar in commercial disputes, but they rarely succeed in the abstract. A panel needs facts, and in a case like this one the most persuasive facts sit inside the claimant’s own trading records, compliance procedures and internal communications. Building the defense from the claimant’s witnesses meant the key propositions were not disputed opinions from an outside expert. They were admissions about the firm’s knowledge and choices.

That approach requires command of technically demanding material: securities account mechanics, the timing rules under Regulation SHO, the difference between a locate and an actual borrow, and the way a fail-to-deliver position ages. Mr. Ni’s training in securities class action practice involved this kind of record, in which liability and damages turn on reconstructing what market participants knew at specific moments.

Points for customers facing broker-dealer claims

Several practical points follow for investors and companies on the receiving end of a FINRA claim from their own broker. First, the contractual allocation of risk is the beginning of the analysis, not the end. Account agreements do not usually authorize a firm to enlarge a loss through its own timing decisions and then pass the full amount to the customer.

Second, regulatory obligations can define the measure of loss. Where a rule such as Regulation SHO dictates when a position must be closed, the cost at that moment is a natural benchmark, and amounts beyond it invite a causation inquiry.

Third, documents and testimony from the claimant are often the strongest evidence available to the respondent. Requests for trading records, compliance escalations and internal communications deserve attention early in the discovery process.
