Securities Litigation Risk Management for Listed Chinese Companies

📅 October 13, 2026
✎ Angus Ni
⌚ 3 min read

A U.S. listing brings capital, visibility and a standing exposure to private securities litigation. For companies headquartered in China, that exposure carries added complications, from cross-border discovery to the difficulty of explaining U.S. procedure to boards and executives who have never faced it. Angus Ni, a New York securities litigator and co-founder of Morrow Ni LLP, spent years on the other side of these cases, prosecuting class actions for institutional investors, and now advises listed companies on how that exposure arises and how to manage it.

The exposure is manageable, but only if it is understood before an adverse event rather than after one.

How the claims arise

Most securities class actions against listed companies rest on a small number of provisions. Section 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act and Rule 10b-5 reach material misstatements or omissions made with scienter in connection with the purchase or sale of securities. For companies that recently went public, Section 11 of the Securities Act imposes liability for material misstatements in a registration statement without any need to prove intent, subject to limited defenses.

The trigger is usually a stock drop following a disclosure: a missed forecast, a short-seller report, a regulatory inquiry or an auditor resignation. A complaint can follow within days, and the Private Securities Litigation Reform Act then sets in motion a lead plaintiff process that tends to favor institutional investors with the largest losses.

Companies that ask the same questions about their own disclosures, before an adverse event, are better placed when one occurs.

The view from the plaintiff’s side

Mr. Ni’s years at Bernstein Litowitz Berger & Grossmann LLP, where he represented pension funds and hedge funds, inform how he approaches defense-side risk. Institutional plaintiffs and their counsel evaluate a case by asking whether the company’s public statements can be matched against internal information that contradicted them, and whether the timing of the stock price reaction supports loss causation.

That means treating disclosure as a litigation document. Risk factors should describe risks that are specific and current, not generic warnings about events that have already happened. Forward-looking statements should be identified as such and accompanied by meaningful cautionary language, which is what the PSLRA safe harbor requires. Statements on earnings calls and in investor meetings carry the same exposure as statements in filings.

Cross-border complications

For China-based issuers, litigation risk does not stop at disclosure. If a case survives a motion to dismiss, discovery may reach documents and witnesses located in China, where data security and personal information protection laws restrict the transfer of certain information abroad. Conflicts between U.S. discovery obligations and Chinese law have to be managed deliberately rather than discovered midway through a case.

Language is a practical risk as well. Key documents, chat messages and internal reports are often in Chinese, and disputes over translation can shape how a court or jury reads intent. Working in both English and Mandarin, Angus can review source documents in the original and prepare witnesses without relying on an interpreter for the most consequential conversations.

Conclusion

A sensible program usually includes a review of disclosure controls with litigation counsel involved, a plan for responding to short-seller reports and regulatory inquiries, an understanding of D&O insurance coverage before it is needed, and a preservation protocol that accounts for cross-border data rules. None of these steps prevents a lawsuit; each changes how one unfolds. Trading disputes raise similar questions of timing and knowledge, as the record in the J.P. Morgan Reg SHO arbitration shows.
